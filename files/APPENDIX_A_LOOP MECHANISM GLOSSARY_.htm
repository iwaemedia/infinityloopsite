Appendix A: Loop Mechanism Glossary

Core Mechanisms

Scope and Use: The four mechanisms below are analytical categories used in this book. They identify recurring functions that can appear in different institutions and eras. Functional similarity does not, by itself, establish identical institutions, a fixed sequence, common intent, or direct administrative descent. Each historical application therefore depends on claim-specific evidence.

Surveillance The systematic monitoring, tracking, observation, or data collection of individuals, groups, places, or activity. In this book, the term refers specifically to documented surveillance practices that targeted Black individuals or communities, or that produced racially disparate exposure to scrutiny. Surveillance may operate through direct observation (patrols, checkpoints, cameras, searches) or informational systems (databases, algorithms, registries, gang lists). In documented cases, surveillance can create or intensify contact, classification, or scrutiny that may feed later criminalization.

Historical manifestations include: slave patrols monitoring enslaved people's movements; overseer records tracking labor and behavior; Jim Crow-era monitoring of Black political organizing; urban police stop-and-frisk programs; predictive-policing systems; facial-recognition systems; social-media monitoring; and automated license-plate readers. These are historically distinct practices. Their inclusion together reflects comparable monitoring functions, not an assertion that they are identical institutions or parts of one uninterrupted administrative chain.

Within the Loop framework, surveillance is most salient when monitoring or classification materially increases exposure to scrutiny, intervention, or consequential decision-making. Criminalization The legal and social processes through which conduct, presence, status, or association is defined or treated as unlawful, sanctionable, or evidence of heightened risk. In this book, criminalization includes both laws that explicitly targeted Black people and facially neutral laws or practices shown through claim-specific evidence to have been enforced or applied in racially disparate ways.

Historical manifestations include: Black Codes criminalizing unemployment and movement; Jim Crow laws enforcing segregation and restricting public conduct; anti-loitering and vagrancy statutes subject to racially disparate enforcement; racially disparate enforcement of drug laws; gang-database classifications that can expose listed individuals to heightened scrutiny or legal consequences; and risk-assessment tools that can influence release, supervision, sentencing, or correctional decisions.

Within the Loop framework, criminalization is most salient when ordinary, low-level, or otherwise noncriminalized conduct is converted into legal jeopardy, punitive exposure, or a consequential risk classification.

Extraction The transfer or depletion of money, property, labor, time, or liberty through the systems examined in this book. Extraction may operate through direct financial or property loss (fines, fees, asset forfeiture, court costs), compelled or undercompensated labor, incarceration or supervision, and documented barriers that reduce access to employment, housing, or wealth-building opportunities.

Historical manifestations include: enslavement and coerced labor; sharecropping systems that extracted agricultural surplus through debt; convict leasing that converted imprisonment into revenue and private economic benefit; discriminatory labor practices; municipal fine and fee systems; civil asset forfeiture; incarcerated labor that in many systems is unpaid or compensated at very low rates; legal financial obligations; and probation or parole supervision fees where authorized. Within the Loop framework, extraction is most salient when criminalization, legal status, or supervision produces material or temporal costs. Economic incentives may reinforce particular practices, but the existence and strength of those incentives must be demonstrated in the specific system being analyzed.

Destabilization The documented disruption or erosion of social, economic, familial, physical, or psychological foundations that support individual and community wellbeing. Destabilization may operate through family separation, employment barriers, housing precarity, health harms, psychological stress or trauma, and community fragmentation.

Historical manifestations include: family separation through slave sales; lynching terror and community violence; migration from the Jim Crow South driven in part by efforts to escape racial violence and oppression while pursuing economic and educational opportunity; redlining and discriminatory lending that restricted access to mortgage credit and contributed to durable housing and wealth disparities; mass incarceration removing adults from households and communities; driver's-license suspensions that can impede mobility and access to employment; eviction or housing instability following court debt; and documented stress, trauma, or chilling effects associated with discrimination, policing, or intensive surveillance.

Within the Loop framework, destabilization is most salient when documented consequences heighten precarity or diminish the capacity to recover, organize, or maintain stability. Any claim that such conditions caused renewed surveillance or criminalization must be supported separately.

The Loop Circuit

The four mechanisms can operate in sequence and in feedback relationships. This circuit is an analytical model, not a claim that every case follows the same order. Mechanisms may overlap, recur, operate in different sequences, or appear without every stage.

Surveillance → Criminalization Monitoring or classification can produce contact or information that, in documented cases, contributes to stops, citations, arrests, charges, designations, or heightened scrutiny.

Criminalization → Extraction A charge, conviction, legal status, or supervision condition can trigger fines, fees, confinement, labor, property loss, or other material and temporal costs.

Extraction → Destabilization The loss of money, property, labor, time, or liberty can disrupt employment, housing, family relationships, health, and psychological wellbeing.

Destabilization → Re-Surveillance/Re-Criminalization Housing instability, unemployment, visible poverty, or continued supervision can become bases for renewed police or administrative contact in some jurisdictions and circumstances.

The circle may recur. The Loop framework is used to test whether such feedback is actually documented in a given case.

Secondary Terms

Adaptive Persistence The book's term for the recurrence of comparable control functions across changes in technology, law, ideology, and institutional form. Functional similarity alone does not prove common intent, identical operation, or direct institutional descent.

Algorithmic Bias Systematic error, differential performance, or differential outcomes associated with a mathematical model, computer system, data set, design choice, or deployment context that can disadvantage particular groups. The existence, direction, and magnitude of bias must be evaluated for the specific system and use case. Carceral Logic An analytical and normative term for a policy orientation that favors policing, surveillance, punishment, confinement, or coercive supervision as responses to social problems over preventive, supportive, restorative, or care-centered approaches.

Carceral State The network of governmental institutions, laws, policies, and related private actors involved in policing, adjudication, detention, incarceration, supervision, surveillance, and punishment. The term is used analytically and does not imply that every institution within the network has the same purpose or function.

Colorblind Racism A scholarly framework describing ways racial hierarchy may persist through ostensibly race-neutral language, rules, or practices. Racially disparate outcomes can be relevant evidence, but disparity alone does not establish discriminatory intent or prove the framework in a particular case.

Digital Redlining The use or deployment of digital systems or automated tools in ways that unlawfully exclude, steer, or disadvantage people or neighborhoods in housing, credit, employment, or services. The term draws an analogy to historical redlining, but the evidence for any modern application must be evaluated independently.

Legal Financial Obligations (LFOs) Fines, fees, court costs, restitution, and other monetary obligations imposed through the criminal legal system. LFOs can create or compound debt and, depending on jurisdiction and enforcement practice, can carry collateral consequences through collection, supervision, licensing, or additional court action.

Pretextual Stop A law-enforcement encounter supported by an observed legal violation but undertaken at least in part to investigate an unrelated suspected offense. Under constitutional doctrine, an objectively valid legal basis may make the stop lawful even when an officer has an additional investigative motive; not every stop for a minor violation is pretextual. Predictive Policing The use of data analysis or statistical tools to anticipate locations, times, types of crime, or other risk indicators and to inform law-enforcement deployment or priorities. When such systems rely heavily on historical enforcement-generated data, they can reproduce earlier enforcement patterns or create feedback effects; the extent of those effects depends on the data, model, policy, and deployment.

Risk Assessment Algorithm A statistical or computational instrument used to estimate specified outcomes such as failure to appear, rearrest, recidivism, or violation of supervision. Such tools may inform pretrial, sentencing, correctional, or supervision decisions. Research has identified validity, transparency, disparate-impact, and fairness concerns for particular tools, but those findings should not be generalized to every instrument.

Surveillance Capitalism A scholarly term most closely associated with Shoshana Zuboff for business models centered on collecting and analyzing behavioral data in order to predict, influence, or monetize human activity. It is not a legal category and does not describe every data-driven business.

Trauma-Informed An approach to policy, practice, or care that recognizes the widespread impact of trauma, recognizes signs and symptoms, integrates knowledge about trauma into policies and practices, seeks to resist re-traumatization, and supports safety and recovery.
