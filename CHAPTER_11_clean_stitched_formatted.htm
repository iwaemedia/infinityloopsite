Chapter 11: The Abolition Horizon: From Evidence to Abolition

2025 and Beyond

Author's Final Loop Impact Rating: 10/10

Author's Qualitative Mechanism Ratings

The Loop rating is an interpretive synthesis, not a validated quantitative index. It summarizes the documented intensity and institutional integration of Surveillance, Criminalization, Extraction, and Destabilization during this era. Every numerical claim below is tied to a source-specific record and is not presented as a universal measure.

  • Surveillance: 10/10 (facial recognition, automated license-plate readers, social-media monitoring, databases, data brokers, and expanding information-sharing capacity)
  • Criminalization: 9/10 (municipal-code enforcement, pretextual stops, risk assessment, gang and association databases, public-camping restrictions, and supervision conditions)
  • Extraction: 9/10 (fines and fees, restitution, forfeiture, private probation, prison labor, commercial services, lost earnings, and family-paid costs)
  • Destabilization: 10/10 (detention, debt, housing and employment insecurity, family disruption, surveillance-related stress, and repeated exposure to enforcement)

Loop Snapshot

  • Surveillance: Digital surveillance has expanded through facial recognition, automated license-plate readers, social-media monitoring, databases, and data brokers, but adoption, retention, interoperability, and oversight vary across jurisdictions. Predictive-policing systems use historical and contextual data to estimate locations or people labeled as higher risk; they do not provide algorithmic precognition.
  • Criminalization: Municipal codes, pretextual traffic and pedestrian stops, gang databases, risk-assessment tools, and supervision rules can convert observation, poverty, association, or technical noncompliance into further legal exposure. The pathway is probabilistic rather than automatic.
  • Extraction: Fines, fees, restitution, forfeiture, private supervision, incarceration-related services, and prison labor create distinct revenue and labor mechanisms. They are not one harmonized national total and should not be ranked as a proven peak of historical economic efficiency.
  • Destabilization: Policing, incarceration, court debt, and surveillance are associated with stress, anxiety, trauma-related symptoms, material instability, housing insecurity, employment barriers, and family strain in affected populations. The evidence does not support universal PTSD or total population-level destabilization.

Introductory Overview

The Kerner Commission concluded in 1968 that the nation was moving toward “two societies, one black, one white,” and characterized those societies as “separate and unequal.”

This concluding chapter synthesizes the documented operation of the Infinity Loop and then presents the author’s abolitionist policy horizon. The ledger contains laws, official investigations, technical reports, court records, statistical measures, scholarly findings, and clearly identified gaps.

The evidence does not form one mathematically complete account of every jurisdiction, person, or dollar. It supports the author’s conclusion that surveillance, criminalization, extraction, and destabilization repeatedly interact across distinct legal and historical settings.

Abolition is presented here as the author’s normative conclusion, not as the only proposition logically permitted by the historical record. The argument rests on the documented limits of selected reforms, the recurring adaptation of punitive systems, and the possibility of building institutions centered on housing, health, care, economic security, accountability, and democratic control.

Major Case Studies

The Ferguson Municipal Circuit

The Justice Department’s Ferguson report was an official federal civil-rights investigation and findings document, not a criminal indictment or judicial judgment. It found that Ferguson’s law-enforcement practices were shaped by the city’s focus on revenue rather than public-safety needs.

City officials pressed police leadership to increase ticketing and collections. The report documented constitutional violations, racial disparities, warrants, added charges, and collection practices.

Ferguson’s court practices could produce added charges, warrants, arrest, and other collateral consequences when residents missed court or could not satisfy obligations. Individual pathways varied, and the Justice Department did not state that every case followed one automatic sequence.

For fiscal year 2015, Ferguson projected $3.09 million in fines and fees, equal to 23.3% of projected general-fund revenue.

The Justice Department documented revenue motive, unconstitutional practices, and racial disparity. The further conclusion that disproportionate impact was the ultimate purpose of the system is the author’s interpretation and is identified as such.

Robert Williams and Nijeer Parks

Detroit police wrongfully arrested Robert Williams after a false facial-recognition lead and detained him for approximately thirty hours. The false lead was compounded by flawed human review, identification, and investigative procedures.

Williams’s detention and the effect on his family are documented. Specific employment losses or other quantified damages require pleadings, testimony, or settlement records and are not assumed.

Nijeer Parks spent ten days in jail after police relied on a false facial-recognition match.

These cases demonstrate that algorithmic error does not remove human responsibility. Detectives, supervisors, identification procedures, warrant applications, prosecutors, and courts remain decision points.

Los Angeles Municipal Code § 41.18 and Grants Pass

The current Los Angeles code regulates sitting, lying, sleeping, obstruction, and property placement in specified public spaces. Its subsections use different location rules. Some designated parks or libraries may be covered up to a stated maximum distance only after council action, signage, and a waiting period; schools and day-care centers are addressed separately.

Most violations are enforced through infractions or administrative citations, with enhanced consequences for specified willful resistance or refusal. The ordinance regulates conduct and locations rather than expressly making homelessness itself a status offense, although it disproportionately affects people who lack private shelter.

In City of Grants Pass v. Johnson, the Supreme Court held in 2024 that enforcing generally applicable public-camping laws does not violate the Eighth Amendment’s Cruel and Unusual Punishments Clause. The ruling left other constitutional and statutory challenges open.

The 1865 Mississippi vagrancy law and modern public-space ordinances are historically and legally distinct. The author compares them through the Loop framework without claiming that their text, racial explicitness, penalties, or constitutional context are identical.

Wrongful Convictions and Legal Eliminations

Wrongful arrest, pretrial detention, erroneous risk classification, unreliable identification, and inability to pay can eliminate liberty and opportunity even when a person is not ultimately convicted. These harms are serious without treating every failure as proof of one uniform institutional intent.

Among defendants who did not recidivate, ProPublica found higher-risk false-positive classifications for approximately 45% of Black defendants and 23% of white defendants in its Broward County analysis.

Risk-assessment tools are used in some pretrial, sentencing, probation, and parole contexts, but their role and legal weight differ by jurisdiction. The phrase “high-tech phrenology” is the author’s critical analogy, not a technical description of the software.

Targeted surveillance can increase police contact, and some contacts are converted into citations, arrests, charges, or risk designations. Charges can produce detention, fines, fees, supervision, conviction, or other burdens even when a case does not result in imprisonment.

Court debt and incarceration can destabilize employment, housing, credit, caregiving, and family relationships. Homelessness, license suspension, unstable supervision, and visible poverty can then create additional exposure to enforcement, but recurrence is not inevitable.

Systematic Pattern Analysis: The Evidence Ledger

The following circuit is the author’s cross-era synthesis, not a nationally measured certainty scale. Each arrow represents a documented pathway whose frequency and intensity depend on the law, agency, jurisdiction, population, and available alternatives.

A. Surveillance → Criminalization

When predictive systems rely heavily on historical enforcement data, they can reproduce and amplify patterns created by prior policing. System design, training data, purpose, and oversight vary.

Pretextual traffic and pedestrian stops remain an important pathway into searches, citations, and arrests. Some gang databases and social-media-monitoring systems use association data to identify or prioritize people for review.

Sarah Brayne’s research supports a broader analysis of data-driven police surveillance.

B. Criminalization → Extraction

Fines, fees, restitution, and related obligations can generate long-term debt and collateral consequences, with rules varying by jurisdiction.

Civil forfeiture can allow government to retain property without obtaining a criminal conviction against the owner. Civil, administrative, and criminal forfeiture are distinct procedures and should not be collapsed into one category.

In documented jurisdictions, private probation companies charged monthly supervision fees and used the threat of jail or revocation to collect debt.

Many incarcerated workers receive pennies per hour or no pay, depending on the jurisdiction and work assignment.

The U.S. Parole Commission’s regulations do not describe the universal modern federal release system. Federal parole was largely abolished for post-1987 federal offenses, and the regulations principally govern legacy and specified categories.

Section 2.40 incorporates general release conditions by reference and authorizes special conditions; subsection (c) addresses drug testing in connection with drug-treatment conditions. It does not establish a general supervision fee or require the parolee to pay drug-testing costs.

Under § 2.52, monetary noncompliance may be considered in revocation, but revocation for specified financial conditions may not be ordered unless financial circumstances are considered and the parolee deliberately evaded or refused compliance.

C. Extraction → Destabilization

Legal debt, incarceration, and conviction can reduce earnings, destabilize housing, interfere with employment, disrupt caregiving, and burden family relationships. The evidence includes causal, longitudinal, and associational studies with different strengths and limits.

A 2020 Brennan Center analysis estimated that formerly imprisoned people lost approximately $55.2 billion in annual earnings, based on 2017 data and modeling assumptions. This is a modeled lost-earnings estimate, not government revenue and not a component that can be added automatically to forfeiture or prison-labor output.

Housing discrimination, segregation, appraisal bias, incarceration, eviction, and neighborhood disinvestment have been major sources of Black wealth loss.

Psychosocial and health burdens are discussed separately from fiscal revenue, procurement, forfeiture, and labor output. The system creates revenue streams and institutional incentives for some public and private actors while also imposing substantial costs on governments, families, and communities.

D. Destabilization → Renewed Criminalization

License suspension for non-driving-related debt can create barriers to work and legal driving. Jurisdiction-specific laws and outcomes are required before stating arrest or charge rates.

Criminalization of homelessness is widespread but varies by ordinance, enforcement practice, available shelter, and state or federal law.

Electronic monitoring can widen supervision when imposed on people who otherwise would not be incarcerated or when technical violations lead to detention. Its effects depend on eligibility, substitution, fees, equipment rules, and revocation standards.

Virginia Eubanks’s research on automated public-benefit systems documents how administrative technology can intensify surveillance and punitive decision-making for poor people.

Institutional Analysis

Measurement Limits and Institutional Opacity

The ledger distinguishes national, state, municipal, program-specific, and case-specific claims. Jurisdictions differ in law, reporting, data definitions, enforcement practice, fiscal structure, and technology adoption.

Missing fields, inconsistent reporting, inaccessible contracts, nondisclosure agreements, proprietary systems, and uneven demographic data reduce precision and accountability. Data gaps can protect institutions from scrutiny regardless of whether every omission was intentionally designed.

Some limitations weaken causal precision; others limit generalization; still others reveal institutional opacity.

Legal Evidence

Los Angeles Municipal Code § 41.18 is a current example of location-based public-space regulation. In the online codification consulted in August 2026, different subsections apply different distance and designation rules. Some provisions require City Council designation, posted notice, and a waiting period, while subsection (e) separately addresses schools and day-care centers. Most violations are infractions or administrative citations, with enhanced consequences for specified willful resistance or refusal.

In City of Grants Pass v. Johnson, 603 U.S. 520 (2024), the Supreme Court held that enforcement of generally applicable public-camping laws does not violate the Eighth Amendment’s Cruel and Unusual Punishments Clause. The decision did not resolve every possible constitutional or statutory challenge to public-space enforcement.

The electronic Code of Federal Regulations provisions at 28 C.F.R. §§ 2.40 and 2.52 govern defined parole and revocation contexts rather than all modern federal supervision. Section 2.40 authorizes conditions of release and addresses drug testing in specified treatment circumstances; § 2.52 requires consideration of financial circumstances in specified revocation decisions involving monetary noncompliance.

The Justice Department’s 2015 Ferguson report is an official civil-rights investigation and findings record. It documents unconstitutional police and court practices, racial disparities, and revenue incentives, but it is not a criminal indictment or judicial judgment.

Mental Health Correlation

Research links police exposure to anxiety, depression, distress, and trauma-related symptoms in specific populations. Among young urban men in one study, more intrusive police contact was associated with higher anxiety and trauma symptoms.

Court debt can generate chronic stress, financial strain, and fear of sanctions. Persistent surveillance-related worry or stress is discussed without treating “ambient anxiety” as a clinical diagnosis.

“Anticipatory conformity” is the author’s term for behavioral self-monitoring under perceived surveillance. It is analytical language rather than a recognized clinical diagnosis.

Intergenerational effects should be discussed through family narratives, repeated exposure, material conditions, institutions, culture, and stress. Epigenetic claims require precise human evidence and are not used as a universal mechanism.

Substance Abuse Analysis

Structural stress, trauma, deprivation, treatment access, drug supply, social networks, and individual factors interact in shaping substance-use risk. Policing intensity alone does not establish the prevalence or cause of a substance-use disorder.

In 2010, Black people were 3.73 times as likely as white people to be arrested for marijuana possession nationwide. Later ACLU analysis found that the disparity remained severe at approximately 3.6 to 1; the national ratio did not necessarily increase.

Black and white marijuana-use rates were comparable in the ACLU analysis. Broader drug-use comparisons must be made substance by substance.

Netherland and Hansen document how the opioid crisis was more often framed through treatment and victimhood than earlier drug crises associated with Black communities, while punitive and public-health responses continued to overlap.

Decriminalization, treatment, housing, and economic support are presented as the author’s recommended strategy.

Resistance and Resilience Strategies: The Abolitionist Horizon

The following program is the author’s policy platform. Its recommendations are not presented as facts already implemented nationwide or as results guaranteed by the historical record.

1. Limit and Democratically Control Surveillance

San Francisco and Boston enacted municipal restrictions on city use of face-surveillance technology under their respective ordinances. These local precedents demonstrate that governments can restrict or prohibit specific systems.

The author recommends a federal moratorium on government facial recognition until constitutional, accuracy, independent-testing, transparency, retention, and redress standards are established. Other surveillance systems should be evaluated technology by technology rather than treated as technically identical.

Automated license-plate readers, social-media monitoring, gang databases, data-broker purchases, and real-time crime centers require public inventories, contract disclosure, retention limits, independent review, access controls, error remediation, and meaningful remedies.

Body cameras can support accountability while also expanding police-controlled video collection. Their effect depends on activation rules, officer access, public disclosure, retention, discipline, and independent enforcement.

2. Replace Police Response Where Care Is the Better Tool

CAHOOTS historically demonstrated a long-running unarmed crisis-response model in Eugene. A 2019 analysis estimated that the program diverted 5% to 8% of Eugene police calls. A 2021 update reported that approximately 2% of CAHOOTS-only calls required police backup.

CAHOOTS ceased serving Eugene effective April 7, 2025. Current recommendations must examine successor services, workforce stability, funding, dispatch integration, service coverage, and long-term sustainability rather than presenting the former program as a currently operating model.

Violence-interruption and community-safety programs should be evaluated individually, with favorable, null, and adverse findings reported rather than assuming one universal effect.

3. End Punitive Poverty and Debt

The author recommends ending offender-funded private probation, unnecessary supervision fees, incarceration for inability to pay, and driver-license suspensions unrelated to highway safety.

Ability-to-pay assessment, notice, counsel, alternatives to payment, debt relief, and judicial review should be built into monetary-sanction systems.

Civil forfeiture should require strong procedural protections, transparent reporting, and separation between enforcement decisions and agency financial benefit. The chapter’s recommendation is informed by documented forfeiture practices and is distinct from these descriptive forfeiture benchmarks.

4. End Coerced and Underpaid Prison Labor

The author recommends eliminating forced labor as punishment, paying fair wages, protecting workplace rights, and clearly accounting for prison-industry goods, prison-maintenance services, work-release arrangements, and supply-chain benefits.

The ACLU/GHRC estimates document substantial goods and service output, but they do not establish one corporate-profit figure or one additive extraction total.

5. Decriminalize Survival and Invest in Social Provision

The author recommends housing, health care, income support, education, transportation, treatment, and community-based crisis response as prevention strategies.

Recommendations involving drug and sex-work decriminalization are policy positions. They should be evaluated through specific statutes, public-health evidence, labor conditions, coercion risks, and program outcomes.

6. Build Accountable Community Safety

Transformative justice, restorative practices, violence interruption, survivor support, and community accountability should be studied program by program. Abolition does not mean the absence of responsibility; it proposes changing the institutions, incentives, and conditions through which safety and accountability are pursued.

Universal social provision is presented as the author’s prevention strategy. Program-specific evidence is required for the effect of housing, income, education, treatment, and health interventions on violence, arrest, or incarceration.

Cultural and Psychological Effects

The cultural effects of the Loop include the repeated use of public-order, criminality, risk, and technical-objectivity narratives to explain or legitimize institutional choices. The chapter treats continuity as functional and comparative: similar functions can recur through historically distinct laws and technologies without proving identical intent or one uninterrupted administrative chain.

Resistance traditions provide counter-narratives and collective memory through independent media, music, visual art, religious institutions, public witness, legal advocacy, and community archives. Psychological effects are described through documented stress, anxiety, trauma-related symptoms, agency, solidarity, and self-censorship where evidence supports them; the chapter does not diagnose entire populations or treat cultural resilience as proof that institutional harms are harmless.

Cross-Chapter Thematic Integration

The post-Civil War Black Codes, Jim Crow, political surveillance, COINTELPRO, mandatory sentencing, data-driven policing, algorithmic classification, and modern public-space restrictions are historically distinct. The Infinity Loop framework compares how they may connect observation, legal classification, resource loss, and social destabilization.

The American Colonization Society’s constitution stated a purpose of colonizing free people of color outside the United States. The text does not itself calculate labor extraction or prove one unified economic motive; those are separate historical interpretations.

Some reforms have been narrowed, repurposed, or accompanied by new forms of control. Others have produced durable reductions in harm. Body cameras, for example, can support accountability while also expanding police-controlled video collection.

The author’s abolition argument is grounded in the documented limits and outcomes of particular reforms rather than in the 10/10 rating alone.

Spirituals are described as religious, emotional, communal, and sometimes communicative forms. Specific route instructions are omitted without primary evidence. Hip-hop has often provided influential critiques of policing, incarceration, inequality, and state power alongside many other themes.

Any reference to MOVE must identify the specific Philadelphia organization, event, or broader movement intended.

Conclusion and Wrap-Up

What the Evidence Establishes

Statutes, regulations, official investigations, court records, program analyses, technical studies, and scholarly research document recurring pathways from surveillance and contact to criminalization, extraction, and material or psychological instability.

The strongest evidence concerns defined statutes, identifiable programs, specific cases, official budgets, technical tests, and dated population measures. The weakest areas are complete national surveillance exposure, harmonized fines-and-fees totals, comparable technology spending, long-term cascading causal effects, and authenticated quotations from secondary books.

What Remains Unfinished

The unfinished archive should include program evaluations, contracts, local laws, technical documentation, litigation records, community oral histories, budgets, and measurable outcomes for alternative institutions.

The Abolitionist Conclusion

The author concludes that abolition offers the most coherent response to the documented limits of punitive systems and the repeated emergence of new control mechanisms. This is a normative conclusion, not a mathematical result that excludes every competing policy position.

Abolition is presented as institution-building: creating durable systems of housing, health, education, income, care, accountability, democratic control, repair, and safety that reduce reliance on surveillance, punishment, coercion, and extraction.

Surveillance capacity and data integration have expanded substantially without achieving literal near-total national penetration. The criminalization and adaptation comparisons are the author’s historical interpretation.

Forfeiture, prison-labor output, legal debt, lost earnings, and family costs must remain separately defined rather than being summed into a single multi-billion-dollar transfer.

The system produces substantial psychological, social, economic, and health burdens, with intensity varying across people and places. The final rating supports the chapter’s argument but does not itself prove the necessity of abolition.
