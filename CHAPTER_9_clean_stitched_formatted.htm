Chapter 9: The Automated Loop: BIE Labels, AI, and the Future of Control

2017 – 2024

Author's Qualitative Loop Assessment: 10/10

Introductory Overview

The period from 2017 through 2024 marked a major expansion in scalable biometric, location, and data-driven surveillance. The defining change was not the disappearance of human decision-making. Agencies and companies still selected data, set objectives, purchased systems, interpreted outputs, and imposed consequences. Automation could nevertheless increase speed, reach, opacity, and institutional distance from the resulting harm.

The era began with the FBI’s August 2017 intelligence assessment using the term “Black Identity Extremists.” The assessment framed a claimed subset of ideologically motivated violence through a new intelligence label. Civil-liberties advocates objected that its language could sweep protected political activity into a national-security framework. The assessment was not a statute, a criminal charge, or a formal legal designation of all Black activism as terrorism.

Facial recognition, predictive policing, geofence warrants, commercial data systems, social-media monitoring, and real-time crime centers developed through different agencies, vendors, contracts, and legal authorities. They should not be treated as one seamless national system.

The author’s 10/10 rating describes the Loop framework’s assessment of scalability, opacity, institutional integration, and potential harm. It is not an independently validated index of total coverage or a claim that every jurisdiction adopted the same technology.

Major Case Studies

Case Study 1: The Black Identity Extremist Assessment

The FBI’s August 3, 2017 assessment created and used the phrase “Black Identity Extremists Likely Motivated to Target Law Enforcement Officers.” It described a claimed subset of ideologically motivated violence and linked the category to perceptions of police brutality and racial injustice.

Rakem Balogun

Rakem Balogun became the first widely reported prosecution publicly associated with the FBI’s BIE assessment, although the formal charges were federal firearms offenses. He was denied bail, spent five months in custody, and was released after the charges were dismissed.

Balogun’s case demonstrates the difference between an intelligence label and a criminal offense. The BIE phrase did not itself appear as a statutory charge, but the assessment supplied a political and investigative context that raised civil-liberties concerns.

Case Study 2: Facial Recognition and Wrongful Arrest

Robert Williams

Detroit police wrongfully arrested Robert Williams in January 2020 after a false facial-recognition lead and held him for approximately thirty hours. The false lead initiated an investigation that was compounded by flawed human review, a photo lineup, and identification procedures.

The record does not support the claim that an algorithmic hit alone automatically became probable cause. The wrongful arrest resulted from the interaction of technology, policy, training, and human decision-making.

Litigation concerning Williams documented inadequate policies and safeguards. A June 2024 settlement required Detroit police to treat facial-recognition results only as investigative leads, develop independent and reliable evidence before an arrest, use revised identification procedures, train personnel, and audit prior cases in which facial recognition contributed to an arrest or arrest warrant.

Michael Oliver and Nijeer Parks

Michael Oliver was wrongfully arrested after a facial-recognition misidentification.

Nijeer Parks spent ten days in jail after police relied on a false facial-recognition match. These cases demonstrate how an investigative lead can become a wrongful arrest when officers fail to corroborate it with reliable independent evidence.

Technical Performance and Task Definition

NIST’s 2019 face-recognition testing found substantial demographic differentials. False-positive rates often varied by factors of ten to more than one hundred across groups, although performance varied widely by algorithm, image type, and dataset.

Gender Shades examined commercial gender-classification systems rather than one-to-many identity recognition. It found error rates as high as 34.7% for darker-skinned women and no higher than 0.8% for lighter-skinned men. The highest reported error was roughly forty-three times the lowest within the tested systems, but that derived comparison should not be generalized to all facial-recognition products.

In a 2018 ACLU test, Amazon Rekognition incorrectly matched twenty-eight members of Congress to mugshots. Members of color were approximately 20% of Congress but nearly 40% of the false matches. Those percentages describe the composition of Congress and the false-match set, not group-specific error rates of 5% and 39%.

In 2019, the Government Accountability Office reported that more than 641 million photos were available across repositories searchable through FBI FACE Services. This was not the measured endpoint of the same database described in Georgetown’s separate 2016 estimate involving more than 117 million U.S. adults.

Case Study 3: Predictive Policing and Feedback Loops

A 2016 Oakland simulation by Kristian Lum and William Isaac showed how predictive policing trained on recorded drug-enforcement data could reproduce and amplify geographic and racial disparities. The modeled deployment would have disproportionately targeted lower-income communities and Black residents even though survey evidence suggested drug use occurred throughout the city.

The simulation demonstrated a feedback mechanism rather than a causal effect established in every city.

The Los Angeles Police Commission’s Office of the Inspector General found that inadequate data, weak controls, and limited documentation prevented a reliable assessment of selected LAPD data-driven policing programs.

Predictive systems do not create future crime knowledge. They classify locations or people from historical and proxy data selected by institutions. Human actors determine the inputs, objectives, deployment rules, review standards, and consequences.

Case Study 4: Geofence Warrants and Location Data

Geofence warrants require a technology company to search stored location information for devices detected within a defined place and time. They can expose data associated with people who were present but uninvolved in the investigated offense.

Google reported that geofence warrants had grown to more than 25% of all U.S. warrants it received. Published counts rose from 941 in 2018 to 11,033 in 2020.

Derrick Ingram’s case concerned an NYPD siege and arrest following a protest-related allegation, not a geofence-warrant identification.

In Carpenter v. United States, the Supreme Court held that government acquisition of historical cell-site location information was a Fourth Amendment search and generally required a probable-cause warrant. The Court described its holding as narrow and did not decide the constitutionality of geofence warrants, tower dumps, real-time tracking, or every digital-location technique.

Case Study 5: Algorithmic Risk Assessment and Decision Systems

COMPAS and Inherited Legal Precedent

ProPublica’s Broward County analysis reported that among defendants who did not recidivate, 45% of Black defendants and 23% of white defendants were classified as higher risk. In the controlled analysis, Black defendants were estimated to be 77% more likely than comparable white defendants to receive a higher violent-recidivism score and 45% more likely to receive a higher general-recidivism score.

These are different statistical measures and denominators. The 77% result is not a false-positive rate. State v. Loomis was decided by the Wisconsin Supreme Court in 2016 and therefore belongs primarily in Chapter 8. As inherited precedent, Loomis permitted sentencing courts to consider COMPAS only with specified limitations and cautions and emphasized that the score should not determine incarceration or sentence severity.

Case Study 6: Housing Advertisement Delivery

In 2022, the Justice Department settled a Fair Housing Act case requiring Meta to change the machine-learning system used to deliver housing advertisements because the existing delivery system produced disparities involving protected characteristics.

Algorithmic Accountability Proposals

Federal algorithmic-accountability proposals were introduced in multiple Congresses but were not enacted during the chapter period.

Wrongful Convictions and Legal Eliminations

Facial-Recognition Wrongful Arrests

Robert Williams, Michael Oliver, and Nijeer Parks were not wrongful-conviction cases in the same procedural sense as an exoneration after trial. They are documented legal eliminations through wrongful arrest, detention, prosecution, or loss of liberty after erroneous facial-recognition leads were combined with deficient human investigation. Williams was detained for approximately thirty hours, and Parks spent ten days in jail. The distinction is preserved because an investigative technology can cause severe legal harm without producing a final conviction.

Algorithmic False Positives and Legal Risk

A risk score, facial-recognition candidate, or predictive classification is not itself a conviction. Such outputs can nevertheless alter how a person is investigated, presented in a lineup, assessed for risk, or understood by a decision-maker. Chapter 9 therefore treats automated classifications as potential sources of legal jeopardy whose effect depends on the institutional process that follows, not as automatic proof that every score causes detention or punishment.

Systematic Pattern Analysis

Surveillance to Criminalization

The automated era expanded the persistence and searchability of observations. Facial-recognition repositories, location-data demands, social-media monitoring, real-time crime centers, and intelligence assessments could create investigative leads or classifications that human officials then converted into stops, warrants, lineups, arrests, or other legal action. The record supports this transition in specific documented systems, not a claim that every collected data point becomes a criminal case.

Criminalization to Extraction

When an erroneous identification or data-assisted classification produces arrest, detention, supervision, litigation, or monitoring, the resulting costs include lost liberty, time, family resources, and public expenditures. Vendor contracts also convert surveillance capacity into private revenue. Palantir's reported government revenue demonstrates the scale of one company's government business, but it is not used as a racial-impact measure or combined with unsupported market totals.

Extraction to Destabilization

The Williams and Parks cases document how wrongful arrest and detention can extend beyond the initial encounter into family disruption, work loss, legal expense, and continuing uncertainty. These consequences support the Destabilization mechanism where they are tied to an identified case or source. They are not generalized into unsupported population-wide clinical rates.

Destabilization to Renewed Surveillance

Digital records can persist after the encounter that created them, and uncertainty about retention, sharing, or later interpretation can affect speech, association, and willingness to participate publicly. The chapter treats this as a recurrent institutional risk rather than a universal causal sequence. Renewed surveillance must be demonstrated from the relevant agency, database, policy, or case rather than assumed from distress alone.

Data Feedback

Predictive systems can reproduce feedback when enforcement-generated data are treated as if they were a complete measure of underlying conduct. Lum and Isaac's Oakland simulation demonstrated this mechanism using recorded drug-enforcement data, while the LAPD Inspector General later identified data and documentation weaknesses that limited reliable evaluation of selected data-driven programs. These records support a feedback-risk analysis without proving an identical effect in every jurisdiction.

Institutional Analysis

Ring Partnerships

By 2021, Ring had formed more than 2,000 partnerships with police and fire agencies. These partnerships enabled agencies to request footage through the company’s system; they did not provide unrestricted automatic access to every camera.

Real-Time Crime Centers and Integrated Data

Real-time crime centers expanded across U.S. cities, integrating feeds from cameras, license-plate readers, records systems, and other sources.

Palantir’s role varied by agency, contract, data source, user permission, and governance terms.

Corporate Revenue and Market Claims

Palantir reported 2023 total revenue of $2.225 billion, including $1.222 billion in government revenue. These are companywide Palantir figures and are not combined with Clearview AI into a single annual total.

Clearview AI was founded after 2017 and marketed a scraped-image facial-recognition database to law enforcement and other clients. Comparable companywide financial figures are not publicly available in the cited record, and investigative reporting about international marketing does not establish a court holding.

Any future market estimate must define the product category, firms, accounting period, revenue type, and data source.

Legal Evidence

Carpenter and the Limits of Digital Location Doctrine

Carpenter established constitutional protection for the historical cell-site location information at issue, while expressly leaving many other surveillance techniques unresolved. Courts therefore continue to address geofence warrants and related tools through fact-specific litigation rather than one settled rule covering every digital trace.

Williams v. City of Detroit

Robert Williams sued the City of Detroit and officers over the misuse of facial recognition. He did not bring the reported case titled Williams v. Clearview AI.

Protest Statutes

Florida’s 2021 House Bill 1 expanded riot-related offenses and created civil provisions affecting protest cases. It should not be described as blanket legal protection for violence, and later litigation narrowed the applicable riot definition.

Oklahoma’s 2021 House Bill 1674 created limited civil and criminal immunity for a motorist who unintentionally injured or killed a person while fleeing a riot, exercised due care, and reasonably believed flight was necessary. The conditions are part of the rule and cannot be omitted.

Anti-protest statutes differed substantially by state. Each law requires separate analysis of definitions, penalties, defenses, civil provisions, and judicial interpretation.

Municipal Oversight and Restrictions

Community Control Over Police Surveillance campaigns promoted local approval, disclosure, and use-policy requirements for surveillance technologies. Exact city counts change by date and by what qualifies as a CCOPS-style ordinance.

San Francisco enacted a government-use facial-recognition ban in 2019. Other cities adopted bans or restrictions on different dates and with different scopes.

Mental Health Correlation

Documented wrongful arrests, family accounts, activist testimony, and civil-liberties reporting support discussion of fear, stress, hypervigilance, self-censorship, and uncertainty about how data will be interpreted.

Qualitative descriptions of distress are appropriate when attributed to identified people or documented studies. Clinical-sounding labels such as “ambient authoritarianism” and “surveillance-based developmental trauma” are not used as diagnoses without supporting diagnostic or longitudinal evidence.

Substance Use and Evidence Limits

The cited record does not establish a quantified increase in alcohol consumption, overdose, prescription medication, or treatment need attributable to predictive-policing exposure, camera density, automatic-license-plate-reader deployment, or social-media monitoring.

Stress, criminalization, treatment access, and substance use may interact, but causation requires a defined exposure, population, comparison group, outcome, and adjustment method.

Resistance and Resilience Strategies

Litigation and Public Records

Civil-rights litigation, public-records requests, investigative journalism, technical evaluations, and transparency databases exposed system design and misuse. Williams v. City of Detroit, Parks v. McCormac, the ACLU’s Amazon test, the LAPD Inspector General review, and GAO oversight reports supplied traceable evidence.

Local Democratic Control

CCOPS-style ordinances sought municipal approval, disclosure, and public use policies before agencies acquired or deployed surveillance systems.

Facial-Recognition Restrictions

San Francisco’s 2019 ordinance provided a documented example of local restriction on government facial-recognition use.

Digital Defense and Counter-Surveillance

Organizations including the Stop LAPD Spying Coalition, the Electronic Frontier Foundation, the ACLU, community technologists, and local activists developed digital-defense education, contract scrutiny, public comment, litigation support, and alternative visions of public safety.

Open Technical Critique

Researchers, programmers, and affected communities tested systems, documented error, and challenged the assumption that proprietary or mathematical outputs were neutral. This resistance demonstrated that technical systems could be analyzed, independently tested, limited, or rejected, even when vendors asserted secrecy or trade-secret protection.

Cultural and Psychological Effects

Digital surveillance altered how people understood public space, online speech, association, and political participation. The strongest evidence supports descriptions of chilling effects, family fear, strategic self-censorship, and the burden of uncertainty, not a claim of permanent population-wide psychological or neurological reorganization.

The same technologies also became objects of cultural criticism. Art, journalism, documentary work, scholarship, and community education made facial recognition, data brokerage, predictive policing, and location surveillance visible to broader audiences.

Social trust could be strained when people did not know whether messages, photographs, associates, or locations would become investigative data.

Cross-Chapter Thematic Integration

■ Surveillance: From Physical Observation to Scalable Data Systems

Earlier chapters traced passes, patrols, ledgers, political files, informants, wiretaps, gang databases, and social-media monitoring. Chapter 9 adds scalable biometric search, location-data demands, repository access, platform partnerships, and integrated real-time systems.

■ Criminalization: From Legal Categories to Data-Driven Suspicion

The BIE assessment, predictive models, facial-recognition leads, risk scores, and protest statutes show how suspicion could be produced through intelligence language, statistical models, and administrative classification as well as through traditional criminal law.

■ Extraction: From Coerced Labor and Court Debt to Procurement and Data Markets

The automated era created public contracts, vendor dependence, monitoring markets, data integration, and new forms of paid infrastructure.

■ Destabilization: From Visible Force to Uncertainty and Chilling Effects

Wrongful arrests remained concrete and visible. Other effects operated through uncertainty: people could not always know what data were collected, how long they were retained, how scores were generated, or when an association would be interpreted as risk.

Conclusion

The years 2017-2024 brought a major expansion in biometric, location, and data-driven surveillance. The technologies could operate at scales and speeds impossible in earlier eras, while proprietary systems and complex institutional arrangements could make responsibility harder to trace.

The evidence does not support literal omniscience, a self-operating system without human governance, or a single national market and health total. Adoption remained fragmented, court doctrine imposed some limits, local governments enacted restrictions, and public-interest organizations exposed errors and challenged contracts.

The author interprets the automated era as the Loop’s most scalable configuration because prior institutional choices could be encoded, repeated, and distributed through technical systems. That conclusion is an analytical synthesis rather than a mathematically demonstrated forecast of permanent totalitarian control.
