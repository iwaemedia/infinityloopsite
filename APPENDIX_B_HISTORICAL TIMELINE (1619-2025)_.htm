APPENDIX B: HISTORICAL TIMELINE (1619-2025)

Timeline entries are drawn from primary and secondary sources cited throughout the book. Claim-specific verification of dates, laws, and events is traceable to the works cited in the corresponding chapter sections; this timeline is a synthetic chronology, not a stand-alone evidentiary document.

THE FOUNDATIONS: SLAVERY AND ITS IMMEDIATE AFTERMATH

1619; First documented landing of Africans in English Virginia at Point Comfort. The captives had been taken from a Portuguese slave ship; their precise legal status under early Virginia law remains historically contested.

1662; Virginia enacts a statute providing that a child’s enslaved or free status follows the condition of the mother.

1705; Virginia consolidates major laws governing enslaved people and servants in the 1705 Virginia Slave Code.

1793; Congress enacts the first federal Fugitive Slave Act, creating procedures for the recovery of people claimed as fugitives from slavery.

1831; Nat Turner’s rebellion is followed by intensified restrictions on Black movement, assembly, education, and communication in several Southern states.

1850; Congress enacts a stronger Fugitive Slave Act as part of the Compromise of 1850, expanding federal enforcement mechanisms for the recovery of alleged fugitives from slavery.

1857; Dred Scott v. Sandford: the U.S. Supreme Court holds that Dred Scott could not claim federal citizenship and also holds that Congress lacked authority to prohibit slavery in federal territories.

1863; The Emancipation Proclamation takes effect January 1, declaring enslaved people in designated areas then in rebellion against the United States to be free.

1865; The Thirteenth Amendment is ratified, abolishing slavery and involuntary servitude except as punishment for crime after conviction.

1865; Southern states begin enacting Black Codes regulating the labor, movement, and civil status of freedpeople.

1865; Congress establishes the Bureau of Refugees, Freedmen, and Abandoned Lands (Freedmen’s Bureau).

1865; The first Ku Klux Klan is formed in Pulaski, Tennessee; sources differ on whether its founding is dated late 1865 or early 1866, but federal and Tennessee historical records commonly place its formation in 1865.

CHAPTER 1 ERA: RECONSTRUCTION’S VIOLENT REVERSAL (1865-1877)

1866; Congress enacts the Civil Rights Act of 1866 over President Andrew Johnson’s veto, establishing federal citizenship and equal civil rights protections.

1867-1868; The Reconstruction Acts place the former Confederate states, except Tennessee, under military districts and establish requirements for readmission to congressional representation.

1868; The Fourteenth Amendment is ratified, establishing national citizenship and prohibiting states from denying due process or equal protection of the laws.

1870; The Fifteenth Amendment is ratified, prohibiting denial or abridgment of voting rights on account of race, color, or previous condition of servitude.

1870-1871; Congress enacts Enforcement Acts, including the Ku Klux Klan Act, to protect voting and federal civil rights against organized violence.

1873; Colfax Massacre: white supremacist forces kill more than 100 African Americans near Colfax, Louisiana, in violence connected to a contested election and control of local government.

1875; The Civil Rights Act of 1875 prohibits racial discrimination in inns, public conveyances, theaters, and other places of public amusement; major provisions are later invalidated by the Supreme Court in 1883.

1876; The presidential election between Rutherford B. Hayes and Samuel J. Tilden is disputed.

1877; An informal political settlement associated with the disputed 1876 election is followed by the withdrawal of remaining federal troops protecting Reconstruction governments in the South; 1877 is conventionally treated as the end of Reconstruction.

CHAPTER 2 ERA: THE NADIR (1877-1919)

1881; Tennessee enacts a statewide railroad-segregation statute requiring separate accommodations for white and Black passengers; Tennessee historical sources often identify it as the nation’s first statewide Jim Crow segregation law.

1883; In the Civil Rights Cases, the U.S. Supreme Court invalidates key provisions of the Civil Rights Act of 1875 as applied to private discrimination.

1890; Mississippi’s constitutional convention adopts devices including poll taxes and literacy/understanding requirements that become central to Black voter disfranchisement.

1892; Ida B. Wells expands her anti-lynching journalism and campaign after the lynching of Thomas Moss, Calvin McDowell, and Henry Stewart in Memphis.

1896; Plessy v. Ferguson: the U.S. Supreme Court upholds state-imposed racial segregation under the 'separate but equal' doctrine.

1898; Wilmington, North Carolina: white supremacists overthrow a legitimately elected biracial local government and carry out deadly racial violence.

1900s-1910s; Lynching remains a major instrument of racial terror. Documented annual totals vary substantially by year and source.

1908; Springfield Race Riot in Illinois.

1909; The National Association for the Advancement of Colored People (NAACP) is founded.

1915; D. W. Griffith’s The Birth of a Nation is released, celebrating a romanticized Ku Klux Klan and helping inspire the second Klan’s revival.

1915; Leo Frank is lynched in Georgia.

1917; East St. Louis racial violence kills Black residents and destroys homes and property.

1917; The NAACP organizes the Silent Protest Parade in New York City.

1919; Red Summer: a nationwide wave of racial violence, much of it white-on-Black, erupts in numerous U.S. communities.

CHAPTER 3 ERA: MOBILIZATION AND BACKLASH (1919-1945)

1920; Marcus Garvey’s Universal Negro Improvement Association (UNIA) reaches major international prominence; exact membership estimates vary widely.

1921; The Tulsa Race Massacre devastates Tulsa’s Greenwood district, destroying homes, businesses, churches, and other institutions in one of the most destructive episodes of racial violence in U.S. history.

1923; Rosewood Massacre in Florida destroys much of the predominantly Black community of Rosewood.

1925; A. Philip Randolph and others organize the Brotherhood of Sleeping Car Porters.

1929; The Great Depression begins; Black workers experience severe unemployment and discriminatory labor-market conditions.

1931; The Scottsboro Boys case begins after nine Black teenagers are accused of raping two white women in Alabama.

1933; New Deal programs begin. Some major programs and their local administration excluded or disadvantaged many Black workers, particularly where agricultural and domestic workers were excluded or local segregation shaped access.

1935; Harlem Riot occurs in New York City.

1938; Missouri ex rel. Gaines v. Canada: the U.S. Supreme Court holds that Missouri must provide an in-state legal education to Black residents equal to that provided to white residents.

1941; A. Philip Randolph threatens a mass March on Washington; President Franklin D. Roosevelt issues Executive Order 8802 prohibiting discrimination in defense employment and federal defense programs.

1941; The United States enters World War II after the attack on Pearl Harbor.

1942; The Congress of Racial Equality (CORE) is founded.

1943; Detroit Race Riot.

1943; Zoot Suit Riots in Los Angeles.

1943; Racial violence also occurs in Harlem, Mobile, and Beaumont.

1944; Smith v. Allwright: the U.S. Supreme Court holds that Texas’s white-primary system violates the Fifteenth Amendment.

1945; World War II ends.

CHAPTER 4 ERA: COLD WAR CONTRADICTIONS (1946-1955)

1946; A wave of postwar racial violence targets Black Americans, including returning Black veterans.

1947; President Truman’s Committee on Civil Rights issues To Secure These Rights, documenting civil-rights violations and recommending federal reforms.

1947; CORE and the Fellowship of Reconciliation conduct the Journey of Reconciliation to challenge segregation in interstate bus travel; it is sometimes described as the first Freedom Ride.

1948; Executive Order 9981 establishes a policy of equality of treatment and opportunity in the U.S. armed forces, setting military desegregation in motion.

1948; Shelley v. Kraemer: the U.S. Supreme Court holds that judicial enforcement of racially restrictive housing covenants violates the Fourteenth Amendment.

1950; Sweatt v. Painter and McLaurin v. Oklahoma State Regents weaken the practical foundation of 'separate but equal' in graduate and professional education.

1954; Brown v. Board of Education: the U.S. Supreme Court unanimously holds state-mandated racial segregation in public schools unconstitutional.

1955; Emmett Till is murdered in Mississippi.

1955; The Montgomery Bus Boycott begins after Rosa Parks’s arrest.

CHAPTER 5 ERA: COINTELPRO PEAK (1956-1971)

1956; The FBI begins COINTELPRO, initially targeting the Communist Party; later programs expand to other organizations, including the Black Nationalist-Hate Groups program launched in 1967.

1957; The Little Rock Nine integrate Central High School under federal protection.

1957; Congress enacts the Civil Rights Act of 1957.

1960; The Greensboro sit-ins begin.

1960; The Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee (SNCC) is founded.

1961; Freedom Rides challenge segregation in interstate travel facilities.

1962; James Meredith enrolls at the University of Mississippi under federal protection.

1963; Birmingham Campaign.

1963; March on Washington for Jobs and Freedom; Martin Luther King Jr. delivers the 'I Have a Dream' address.

1963; The 16th Street Baptist Church bombing kills four girls in Birmingham.

1963; Medgar Evers is assassinated in Mississippi.

1964; The Civil Rights Act of 1964 prohibits discrimination in major areas including public accommodations and employment.

1964; Mississippi Freedom Summer.

1964; Malcolm X leaves the Nation of Islam.

1965; Malcolm X is assassinated.

1965; Selma-to-Montgomery voting-rights campaign includes 'Bloody Sunday.'

1965; The Voting Rights Act of 1965 is enacted.

1965; Watts uprising in Los Angeles.

1966; The Black Panther Party is founded in Oakland, California.

1967; The 'Long Hot Summer' includes major urban rebellions and civil disorders in Detroit, Newark, and other cities.

1968; Martin Luther King Jr. is assassinated in Memphis.

1968; The Fair Housing Act is enacted as Title VIII of the Civil Rights Act of 1968.

1968; King’s assassination is followed by civil unrest in more than 100 U.S. cities.

1968; Richard Nixon is elected president after campaigning heavily on 'law and order.'

1969; The FBI intensifies operations against the Black Panther Party and other Black nationalist organizations under COINTELPRO.

1970; Congress enacts the Comprehensive Drug Abuse Prevention and Control Act, including the Controlled Substances Act.

1971; Attica Prison uprising in New York.

1971; President Nixon declares drug abuse 'public enemy number one,' a major milestone in the federal War on Drugs.

CHAPTER 6 ERA: THE MODERN CARCERAL STATE (1972-1989)

1972; Furman v. Georgia invalidates existing death-penalty schemes, temporarily halting executions in the United States.

1973; New York’s Rockefeller Drug Laws establish severe mandatory prison terms for specified drug offenses.

1974; President Richard Nixon resigns.

1976; Gregg v. Georgia and companion cases uphold revised death-penalty statutes, allowing executions to resume under constitutional sentencing procedures.

1978; Regents of the University of California v. Bakke invalidates a fixed racial quota in medical-school admissions while allowing race to be considered as one factor in admissions.

1980; Ronald Reagan is elected president.

1982; The Reagan administration announces an intensified federal War on Drugs strategy.

1984; Congress enacts the Comprehensive Crime Control Act.

1984; The Sentencing Reform Act creates the U.S. Sentencing Commission and a federal sentencing-guidelines system.

1986; The Anti-Drug Abuse Act creates the federal 100:1 crack-to-powder cocaine quantity ratio for key mandatory-minimum penalties.

1987; McCleskey v. Kemp: the U.S. Supreme Court rejects a death-sentence equal-protection challenge based on statewide statistical racial disparities absent proof of discriminatory purpose in the defendant’s case.

1988; The Anti-Drug Abuse Act of 1988 expands federal drug penalties and creates additional drug-control provisions.

1989; Five Black and Latino teenagers later known as the Central Park Five are wrongfully convicted in New York; their convictions are vacated in 2002.

CHAPTER 7 ERA: THE 'COLORBLIND' ERA (1990-2009)

1991; Video records Los Angeles police officers beating Rodney King.

1992; Los Angeles uprising follows the acquittal of officers charged in the Rodney King beating.

1993; Cultural marker: Snoop Dogg releases 'Murder Was the Case' on Doggystyle, a major work associated with the early-1990s gangsta-rap era.

1994; The Violent Crime Control and Law Enforcement Act becomes law.

1994; Federal 'three strikes’ provisions create mandatory life imprisonment for certain repeat serious violent or drug offenders under specified conditions.

1994; The Violent Crime Control and Law Enforcement Act authorizes $9.7 billion for prisons through its state prison-grant provisions.

1996; The Prison Litigation Reform Act restricts prisoner litigation in federal courts.

1996; The Personal Responsibility and Work Opportunity Reconciliation Act reshapes federal welfare policy.

2000; The combined U.S. prison-and-jail population passes roughly two million people. This aggregate includes people held in both prisons and jails.

2001; After the September 11 attacks, the War on Terror begins and federal surveillance and information-sharing authorities expand.

2005; Hurricane Katrina disproportionately harms Black residents of New Orleans and exposes major racial and socioeconomic disparities in evacuation, displacement, and recovery.

2008; Barack Obama is elected the first Black president of the United States.

CHAPTER 8 ERA: DIGITAL AUGMENTATION (2010-2016)

2010; The Fair Sentencing Act reduces the federal crack-to-powder cocaine mandatory-minimum quantity ratio from 100:1 to 18:1 and eliminates the five-year mandatory minimum for simple crack possession.

2012; Trayvon Martin is killed by George Zimmerman in Sanford, Florida.

2013; A federal district court in Floyd v. City of New York finds aspects of the NYPD’s stop-and-frisk practices unconstitutional; the case itself was filed in 2008, not first brought in 2012.

2013; George Zimmerman is acquitted in the killing of Trayvon Martin.

2013; Black Lives Matter emerges after the Zimmerman acquittal, associated with Alicia Garza, Patrisse Cullors, and Opal Tometi.

2013; Kalief Browder is released after nearly three years in pretrial detention at Rikers Island without being convicted.

2014; Michael Brown is killed by police officer Darren Wilson in Ferguson, Missouri.

2014; Eric Garner dies after an NYPD officer places him in a prohibited chokehold during an arrest on Staten Island.

2014; Tamir Rice is fatally shot by Cleveland police.

2014; John Crawford III is fatally shot by police in an Ohio Walmart.

2015; The U.S. Department of Justice releases its Investigation of the Ferguson Police Department on March 4.

2015; Freddie Gray dies after suffering injuries in Baltimore police custody; widespread protests and unrest follow.

2015; Sandra Bland dies in a Texas jail after a traffic stop and arrest.

2015; A white supremacist murders nine Black worshippers at Emanuel AME Church in Charleston, South Carolina.

2016; Philando Castile is fatally shot by a police officer in Minnesota.

2016; Alton Sterling is fatally shot by police officers in Baton Rouge, Louisiana.

2016; State v. Loomis: the Wisconsin Supreme Court permits limited use of the COMPAS risk assessment at sentencing subject to cautions and limitations. The U.S. Supreme Court did not issue this holding; it later denied review.

CHAPTER 9 ERA: THE AUTOMATED LOOP (2017-2024)

2017; An FBI Intelligence Assessment dated August 3 introduces the 'Black Identity Extremist' (BIE) label.

2017; A white-supremacist rally in Charlottesville, Virginia, culminates in the killing of Heather Heyer.

2018; Stephon Clark is fatally shot by Sacramento police.

2018; Carpenter v. United States: the U.S. Supreme Court holds that obtaining at least seven days of historical cell-site location information generally requires a warrant.

2019; Nijeer Parks is wrongfully arrested in New Jersey after police rely on an erroneous facial-recognition lead.

2019; The Government Accountability Office reports that the FBI’s face-recognition office can search databases containing more than 641 million photos.

2020; Breonna Taylor is killed during a Louisville police raid.

2020; George Floyd is murdered by Minneapolis police officer Derek Chauvin, prompting mass protests in the United States and internationally.

2020; Robert Williams is wrongfully arrested in the Detroit area after police rely on an incorrect facial-recognition match.

2020; Racial-justice demonstrations during the COVID-19 pandemic become one of the largest protest mobilizations in modern U.S. history.

2021; Derek Chauvin is convicted of murder and manslaughter in George Floyd’s death.

2021; Los Angeles amends Municipal Code Section 41.18 to prohibit sitting, lying, sleeping, or storing personal property in specified public locations and near designated sensitive uses. The ordinance regulates conduct and location; it does not literally make the status of homelessness a crime.

2021; The January 6 attack on the U.S. Capitol prompts public and scholarly debate over differences in law-enforcement preparation and response across protest movements; the disparity interpretation is treated as analysis rather than a self-proving event fact.

2022; Ketanji Brown Jackson becomes the first Black woman to serve as a justice of the U.S. Supreme Court.

2023; Tyre Nichols dies after a violent encounter with Memphis police officers; federal civil-rights and criminal proceedings follow.

2023; By this period, multiple U.S. jurisdictions have enacted bans or significant restrictions on government or police use of facial-recognition technology. These local policies do not constitute a single nationwide rule.

2024; City of Grants Pass v. Johnson: the U.S. Supreme Court holds that enforcement of generally applicable laws regulating camping on public property does not violate the Eighth Amendment’s Cruel and Unusual Punishments Clause.

2024; Detroit settles Robert Williams’s facial-recognition wrongful-arrest lawsuit and adopts restrictions requiring independent evidence before an arrest may be based on a facial-recognition lead.

CHAPTER 10: RESISTANCE AND RESILIENCE MILESTONES (1619-PRESENT)

1739; Stono Rebellion in South Carolina.

1800; Gabriel Prosser’s planned uprising in Virginia is discovered before it can be carried out.

1811; German Coast uprising in Louisiana, widely described as the largest revolt of enslaved people in U.S. history.

1831; Nat Turner’s rebellion in Virginia.

1839; Captives aboard the Amistad revolt against their captors.

1846; Dred and Harriet Scott sue for their freedom in Missouri; the case reaches the U.S. Supreme Court in 1857.

1847; Frederick Douglass begins publishing The North Star.

1849; Harriet Tubman escapes slavery and reaches freedom in the North.

1850; Tubman begins returning to Maryland to guide family and others to freedom.

1863-1865; Roughly 179,000 Black men serve as soldiers in the U.S. Army during the Civil War, with about 19,000 additional Black sailors serving in the Navy.

1866; Fisk University is founded during the post-Civil War expansion of institutions that would become historically Black colleges and universities.

1892; Ida B. Wells intensifies her anti-lynching campaign after the Memphis lynchings.

1905; The Niagara Movement is founded.

1909; The NAACP is founded.

1920; The Negro National League is established.

1930; The Nation of Islam is founded in Detroit.

1942; CORE is founded.

1954; Brown v. Board of Education produces a landmark legal victory against state-mandated school segregation.

1955-1956; Montgomery Bus Boycott.

1960; SNCC is founded and the student sit-in movement spreads.

1964; Freedom Summer and the Mississippi Freedom Democratic Party challenge racial exclusion in voting and party representation.

1965; Malcolm X is assassinated; his speeches and writings continue to influence Black Power and later political thought.

1966; The Black Panther Party is founded.

1967; Loving v. Virginia invalidates state bans on interracial marriage.

1969; The Black Panther Party expands community 'survival programs,' including the Free Breakfast for Children Program; these programs did not begin at the Party’s 1966 founding.

1972; Shirley Chisholm seeks the Democratic nomination for president, becoming the first Black candidate to seek a major party’s presidential nomination and the first woman to seek the Democratic nomination.

1989; Representative John Conyers introduces H.R. 3745, the first version of the federal Commission to Study Reparation Proposals for African Americans Act that later becomes known by the recurring H.R. 40 designation.

2013; Black Lives Matter emerges as a movement and organizing framework.

2014; Ferguson protests and organizing follow the killing of Michael Brown.

2016; The Movement for Black Lives releases a policy platform.

2020; Mass protests follow George Floyd’s murder. Participation estimates place the mobilization among the largest protest movements in U.S. history, but they do not establish an uncontested single ranking.

2020; California enacts AB 3121, establishing the Task Force to Study and Develop Reparation Proposals for African Americans.

2021; California’s reparations task force holds its inaugural meeting.

2021; Evanston, Illinois authorizes implementation of its Local Reparations Restorative Housing Program; applications open later that year.

2023; Evanston reports distributing more than $1 million through its local reparations program.

2024-2025; State and local reparations proposals, public-safety alternatives, surveillance restrictions, mutual-aid projects, and decriminalization campaigns continue across multiple jurisdictions. These are decentralized developments rather than a single national event.

CHAPTER 11: ABOLITION HORIZON: AUTHORIAL / ASPIRATIONAL (2025 AND BEYOND)

2025-2030; Authorial projection: continued organizing toward an abolitionist horizon.

2025-2030; Authorial projection: expansion of community-based public-safety models.

2025-2030; Authorial projection: growth of mutual-aid networks.

2025-2030; Authorial projection: continued reparations advocacy and legislation.

2025-2030; Authorial projection: additional restrictions on surveillance technologies.

2025-2030; Authorial projection: continued decriminalization efforts.

2030+; Authorial statement: the work continues.
